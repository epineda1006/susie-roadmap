Surgical Pathology

The core of anatomical pathology — most common

▾
What you do every day
Examine tissue biopsies sent from operating rooms and clinics under a microscope. Make diagnoses that directly guide patient treatment — "Is this cancer? What type? How aggressive?" During surgery, frozen sections are sent to you in real-time so surgeons know how much tissue to remove before the patient leaves the OR.
Types of cases
Breast biopsies, colon polyps, thyroid nodules, skin lesions, liver biopsies, appendix specimens, cancer staging specimens from surgeries.
Work setting
Hospital pathology lab, directly adjacent to operating rooms. Close collaboration with surgeons and oncologists.
Why it might be for you
You want to be the expert doctors call when they need answers. Every diagnosis you make directly affects a patient's treatment plan — even if you never see them.
No extra fellowship neededMost common pathology jobHigh demand
🩸

Hematopathology

Blood cancers, bone marrow, lymphoma

▾
What you do every day
Diagnose diseases of the blood and bone marrow. Read blood smears, bone marrow biopsies, and lymph node biopsies. Interpret flow cytometry data to identify abnormal blood cell populations. Make diagnoses of leukemia and lymphoma that determine which chemotherapy a patient receives.
Types of cases
Leukemia (AML, CLL, ALL), lymphoma (Hodgkin, non-Hodgkin), multiple myeloma, myelodysplastic syndrome, severe anemia, platelet disorders.
Work setting
Academic medical centers and large hospitals. Close collaboration with oncology and hematology teams.
Why it might be for you
Hematopathology sits at the intersection of lab medicine and oncology. The diagnoses are complex, the science is rich, and your work directly determines cancer treatment protocols for patients.
1-year fellowship requiredFlow cytometry + molecularGrowing field

Forensic Pathology

Medical examiner — cause and manner of death

▾
What you do every day
Work for the medical examiner or coroner's office. Determine the cause of death (what killed the person) and manner of death (natural, accident, homicide, suicide, undetermined). Perform autopsies. May testify in court as a forensic expert witness. Work closely with law enforcement, attorneys, and families.
Types of cases
Homicides, drug overdoses, traffic fatalities, suicides, sudden infant death, workplace accidents, any death that was unexpected, unexplained, or occurred outside a hospital.
Work setting
County/state medical examiner's office or coroner's office. Government setting — not hospital-based. Unique among pathology specialties.
Why it might be for you
If you want to serve justice and give families answers, this is one of the most meaningful careers in medicine. Every case is a story. You are the last doctor that person will ever see — and your work matters for their family and for the legal system.
1-year fellowship requiredCourtroom testimonyGovernment / public sector
🧠

Neuropathology

Brain, spinal cord, and nervous system diseases

▾
What you do every day
Diagnose diseases of the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves. Interpret brain tumor biopsies taken during neurosurgery (often in real-time frozen sections). Perform neuropathological autopsies on patients with suspected neurodegenerative diseases. Consult with neurosurgeons and neurologists.
Types of cases
Brain tumors (glioblastoma, meningioma), epilepsy surgery specimens, Alzheimer's disease (brain autopsy), MS plaques, spinal cord injuries, peripheral nerve biopsies.
Work setting
Academic medical centers and major hospitals with neurosurgery programs. One of the more specialized settings in pathology.
Why it might be for you
The most intellectually demanding subspecialty in pathology. Combines deep neuroscience knowledge with diagnostic mastery. If brain diseases fascinate you, this is where to be.
2-year fellowship requiredMost academicDeep neuroscience

Dermatopathology

Skin biopsies — melanoma, rashes, cancer

▾
What you do every day
Read skin biopsies under a microscope all day. Diagnose melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, inflammatory skin diseases, hair loss disorders, and skin infections — all from a tiny punch or shave biopsy sent from a dermatologist's office.
Types of cases
Melanoma, psoriasis, eczema/dermatitis, skin infections (fungal, bacterial), alopecia (hair loss), rare skin tumors, drug reactions on skin.
Work setting
Hospital labs, private dermatopathology labs, or dermatology group practices. One of the more autonomous and flexible settings — private practice options are strong.
Why it might be for you
High volume, pattern-recognition based, and surprisingly lucrative. You can enter from either a pathology residency or a dermatology residency. Great work-life balance in private practice.
1-year fellowshipPrivate practice friendlyPattern recognition

Molecular Pathology

DNA, genetics, precision medicine — the future

▾
What you do every day
DNA and RNA-based diagnostic testing. Identify genetic mutations in tumors to guide targeted therapy. Run PCR, next-generation sequencing (NGS), FISH testing, and interpret complex genomic data. Consult with oncologists about which drug will work for a patient based on their tumor's genetics.
Types of cases
BRCA1/2 testing (breast/ovarian cancer), EGFR mutations (lung cancer), KRAS testing (colon cancer), hereditary disease testing, viral load monitoring, pharmacogenomics.
Work setting
Hospital molecular labs, commercial reference laboratories, biotech and pharmaceutical companies. One of the few pathology subspecialties with strong industry options.
Why it might be for you
The fastest-growing subspecialty in all of pathology. Precision medicine runs through molecular pathology. If you're drawn to genetics, technology, and cutting-edge science — this is the frontier of diagnostic medicine.
1-year fellowshipFastest growing fieldBiotech / industry options
💧

Cytopathology

Individual cells — Pap smears, fine-needle aspiration

▾
What you do every day
Examine individual cells rather than tissue sections. Interpret Pap smears for cervical cancer screening, guide fine-needle aspiration (FNA) biopsies of thyroid nodules and lymph nodes, and analyze fluid from around the lungs or in the abdomen for cancer cells. Often present in real-time during procedures to confirm adequate sample was collected.
Types of cases
Cervical cancer screening (Pap smears), thyroid nodule FNA, lung FNA, lymph node FNA, pleural and peritoneal fluid, CSF analysis for meningitis or cancer.
Work setting
Hospital labs and outpatient clinics. Cytopathologists often go to procedure suites to perform rapid on-site evaluations (ROSE) — a very hands-on role compared to other pathology subspecialties.
Why it might be for you
You want to be at the bedside (kind of). Cytopathology is one of the most patient-adjacent subspecialties in pathology — you're often in the room when the biopsy happens. Great for someone who wants pathology but doesn't want to be completely removed from clinical care.
1-year fellowshipMost patient-adjacentHigh demand
🧪

Clinical Pathology / Lab Medicine

Running the hospital lab — chemistry, blood bank, micro

▾
What you do every day
Direct and oversee hospital clinical laboratories — chemistry, hematology, microbiology, blood bank, coagulation, immunology. Interpret unusual lab results, consult with physicians on complex cases, manage lab quality and safety, and make decisions about blood transfusions and test ordering.
Types of cases
Unusual electrolyte patterns, transfusion medicine decisions (which blood product for which patient), positive blood cultures for sepsis, coagulation disorder workups, therapeutic drug monitoring.
Work setting
Hospital clinical laboratories, reference laboratories (Quest, LabCorp leadership roles), blood banks. Strong administrative and leadership component.
Why it might be for you
You want to be the expert behind every lab test a doctor orders. Clinical pathologists run the infrastructure of modern medicine — without the clinical lab, no diagnosis happens. If you like systems, leadership, and breadth over depth, this is your lane.
No extra fellowship neededStrong leadership roleHospital administration
👶

Pediatric Pathology

Diseases in children — developmental, congenital, cancer

▾
What you do every day
Diagnose diseases exclusively in children — from premature newborns to teenagers. Includes pediatric tumors, congenital birth defects, placental pathology, fetal autopsies, and developmental diseases that are completely different from adult conditions.
Types of cases
Wilms tumor (kidney cancer in children), neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma, SIDS investigations, birth defects, placenta pathology in complicated pregnancies, childhood leukemia tissue diagnosis.
Work setting
Children's hospitals and academic medical centers. Highly specialized — only major children's hospitals have dedicated pediatric pathology departments.
Why it might be for you
The most emotionally meaningful subspecialty in pathology. Every diagnosis affects a child and their entire family. The diseases are unique, complex, and require deep specialized knowledge. If children's health drives you — this is where your work will matter most.
1-year fellowshipChildren's hospitals onlyDeeply meaningful
⚕️

Autopsy Pathology

Hospital autopsies — answering the final question

▾
What you do every day
Perform hospital autopsies — requested by families or physicians to understand what exactly caused a patient's death or to confirm a diagnosis. Different from forensic pathology (which is criminal/legal). Hospital autopsies drive quality improvement, answer clinical questions, and sometimes reveal diagnoses that were missed during life.
Types of cases
Hospital patients who died from complex diseases, unexpected deaths in hospitalized patients, cases where physicians want confirmation of their diagnosis, academic autopsy for teaching purposes.
Work setting
Academic hospitals and university medical centers. Most autopsy pathology is part of an anatomical pathology role — it's rarely a standalone subspecialty today.
Why it might be for you
Autopsy pathology gives you the complete picture — the full story of a patient's disease from beginning to end. It's the most intellectually comprehensive work in medicine. Every autopsy teaches something new, and that knowledge improves care for future patients.
Part of AP trainingAcademic settingQuality improvement

Admissions committees read depth, not length. Long commitments beat a list of one-offs.
The MCAT and GPA get you screened in. These experiences get you accepted. Med schools want to see clinical exposure, research, and leadership — especially for competitive schools.

Clinical Experience

Required — minimum 100–200 hours

▾
💡 Pathology-specific clinical experience is a major differentiator. Most pre-meds shadow surgeons or internists — you shadow a pathologist. This shows intentionality.
Shadow a Pathologist (Anatomical or Clinical)
Contact pathology departments at Community Regional Medical Center or UCSF Fresno. Email the department chair directly. Ask for a 1-month shadow experience.

Apply to Clovis Community Medical Center or Saint Agnes Medical Center volunteer programs. Patient interaction shows you understand the human side of medicine.
Clinical Lab / Phlebotomy Volunteer or Work

Medical Scribe (Optional but Powerful)

Research Experience

Strongly recommended — especially for competitive schools

▾
💡 Research experience doesn't require publishing a paper. Showing up consistently for 1–2 semesters, learning techniques, and contributing to a project is enough. Pathology-adjacent research (cancer biology, immunology, histology) is ideal.
Join a Research Lab at Transfer School
Email 5–10 professors in biology, biochemistry, or pathology. Keep the email short: who you are, why you're interested in their work, ask to meet. Do this within the first month of transferring.
Apply to Summer Research Programs (SURF, REU)
NSF-funded REU programs at universities pay you to do summer research. Apply to UC Davis, UCSF, and UCLA programs. Deadlines are usually February. Paid + looks excellent on applications.
Aim for a Poster Presentation or Publication
Presenting research at a campus symposium or conference counts. A published paper is exceptional but not required. Even a poster at an undergraduate research fair is something to list.
Target Pathology or Cancer Biology Labs
Research in tumor biology, histopathology, immunology, or clinical diagnostics directly aligns with your pathology goal. Mention this in your personal statement.

🌡️ A Day in the Life
This is the actual job. Read it when the prerequisites feel abstract.
This is what Susie's actual workday looks like as a fully practicing Physician-Pathologist. No two days are identical — but this is the rhythm. Choose between Anatomical and Clinical pathology days.
🌅

Morning — Slide Reading

7:00 AM – 12:00 PM

▾
7:00 AM
Arrive, coffee, review overnight cases
Check inbox for urgent cases flagged overnight by the on-call team. Prioritize anything marked STAT or from the ICU.
Anatomical
7:30 AM
Gross examination of surgical specimens
Physical examination of organs, tumors, or tissue sent from the OR. Measure, describe, photograph, and dictate findings. Select representative sections for the microscope slides that will be made overnight.
Anatomical
9:00 AM
Microscopy — reading slides from yesterday's cases
Sit at the microscope and examine stained tissue sections. Is this cancer? What type? How aggressive? Write up your diagnostic report. These reports go directly to the surgeon and oncologist treating the patient.
Anatomical
10:30 AM
Frozen section — real-time intraoperative consult
A surgeon calls from the OR: "I need to know if this margin is clear before I close." You receive fresh tissue, freeze it, cut it, stain it, and read it — all in 10–15 minutes. Your answer determines if the patient needs more tissue removed while they're still on the table.
Anatomical
11:30 AM
Multidisciplinary Tumor Board
Meet with oncologists, surgeons, radiologists, and radiation oncologists. You present your pathology findings on each cancer patient — staging, grade, margins. The whole team makes a treatment decision together around your diagnosis.
Collaborative
☀️

Afternoon — Consults & Sign-Outs

12:00 PM – 5:00 PM

▾
12:00 PM
Lunch — often at desk, reviewing cases
Pathologists have more schedule flexibility than most physicians. Lunch is usually calm. No patients to round on.
Downtime
1:00 PM
IHC (immunohistochemistry) review
Yesterday's special stains are ready. You add antibody panels to cases where the diagnosis was unclear — the staining pattern tells you exactly what type of tumor it is. This determines which targeted therapy the patient receives.
Anatomical
2:30 PM
Resident teaching — sign-out sessions
If at an academic center
Pathology residents bring their cases to you and discuss their diagnoses. You teach them what to look for and sign your name confirming the diagnosis. Your signature = legal responsibility.
Teaching
3:30 PM
Clinician phone calls & consults
Surgeons, oncologists, and internists call with questions. "The biopsy came back positive — what does that mean for staging?" You're the expert they turn to. Pathologists are the doctors that other doctors call.
Collaborative
4:30 PM
Final dictations & case sign-out
Complete and sign all reports for the day. Check that all urgent results were communicated. Prepare handoff for the on-call pathologist. Leave by 5–5:30 PM most days — no overnight call once you finish residency.
End of Day
🌅

Morning — Lab Rounds

7:00 AM – 12:00 PM

▾
7:00 AM
Review overnight critical values
Check which patients had critical lab values flagged overnight — dangerously low hemoglobin, positive blood cultures for sepsis, life-threatening electrolyte abnormalities. Ensure physicians were notified.
Clinical Path
7:30 AM
Morning rounds through the clinical labs
Walk through the chemistry, hematology, microbiology, and blood bank sections. Meet with the lab supervisors. Address quality control issues, equipment problems, or unusual results from overnight testing.
Clinical Path
9:00 AM
Blood bank — transfusion review
Review transfusion requests. Approve blood products for surgical patients and ICU. Investigate any transfusion reactions from the previous day. The blood bank is one of the highest-stakes parts of hospital medicine — a transfusion error can kill a patient.
Clinical Path
10:30 AM
Peripheral blood smear review
Hematology auto-analyzers flag unusual cell morphology for human review. You look at the actual blood cells under a microscope — blast cells indicating leukemia, sickle cells, parasites (malaria), or platelet clumping artifacts.
Clinical Path
11:30 AM
Infectious disease microbiology consult
Review positive cultures with the microbiology team and the ID (infectious disease) physicians. Which antibiotic will work? Is this a resistant organism? Your input guides treatment for septic patients in the ICU.
Clinical Path
☀️

Afternoon — Admin & Quality

12:00 PM – 5:00 PM

▾
12:00 PM
Lunch
More flexibility than clinical physicians. No patient rounding during lunch.
Downtime
1:00 PM
Lab quality and accreditation review
Clinical pathologists are responsible for CAP accreditation of the hospital lab. Review quality metrics, turnaround times, error reports, and proficiency testing results. Labs are inspected every 2 years — you keep everything ready.
Admin
2:30 PM
New test evaluation & implementation
Hospitals constantly want to add new lab tests. You evaluate whether a new assay is clinically valid, cost-effective, and technically feasible. You're the gatekeeper of what testing gets offered — and what doesn't.
Strategic
3:30 PM
Clinician education & unusual result calls
Doctors call with complex lab interpretation questions. "What does a 1:640 ANA mean for my patient?" or "Why did the troponin come back this high if it's not a heart attack?" You translate lab data into clinical meaning.
Collaborative
4:30 PM
End of day — handoff and close
Brief the on-call team. Complete documentation. Most clinical pathologists work 9–5 with rare weekend call once you're out of residency.
End of Day

Section 1: Chem/Physics (C/P)

59 questions · 95 minutes · Scored 118–132

▾
📌 Covers gen chem, orgo, physics, biochemistry. Your FCC coursework covers most of this. Biochemistry is highest yield — start there.
Enzyme kinetics — Michaelis-Menten, Km, Vmax, inhibition types
🔴 High Priority
Amino acids — all 20, structures, properties, pKa values
🔴 High Priority
Metabolism — glycolysis, Krebs cycle, ETC, ATP yield
🔴 High Priority
Acids & bases — Henderson-Hasselbalch, buffers, titration curves
🔴 High Priority
Thermodynamics — Gibbs free energy, enthalpy, entropy, spontaneity
🟡 Medium
Electrochemistry — galvanic cells, oxidation/reduction, Nernst equation
🟡 Medium
Organic chemistry reactions — SN1/SN2, E1/E2, carbonyl reactions
🟡 Medium
Physics — circuits, optics, fluids, waves (algebra-based only)
🟡 Medium
Stereochemistry — enantiomers, diastereomers, optical activity
⚪ Lower
Spectroscopy — IR, NMR, mass spec interpretation
⚪ Lower

Section 2: CARS

53 questions · 90 minutes · Scored 118–132

▾
📌 No science content — pure critical reading. Hardest to improve quickly. Start practicing NOW, once a day, every day. This is the section that surprises most pre-meds.
Main idea & author's purpose — identify what the passage is really arguing
🔴 High Priority
Inference questions — what does the author imply but not state directly?
🔴 High Priority
Strengthen/weaken questions — which answer most supports or undermines the argument?
🔴 High Priority
Reading dense humanities texts — philosophy, history, arts, social science
🔴 High Priority
Time management — 9 passages in 90 min = 10 min/passage maximum
🟡 Medium
Tone and rhetoric — identifying how the author feels and persuades
🟡 Medium
🧬

Section 3: Bio/Biochem (B/B)

59 questions · 95 minutes · Scored 118–132

▾
📌 Most directly relevant to pathology. Biochemistry + molecular biology dominate. Know the central dogma cold — DNA → RNA → Protein. Every detail matters here.
DNA replication, transcription, translation — every step and enzyme
🔴 High Priority
Cell cycle — G1/S/G2/M phases, checkpoints, cyclins, CDKs
🔴 High Priority
Genetics — Mendelian ratios, sex-linked, incomplete dominance, codominance
🔴 High Priority
Protein structure — primary through quaternary, folding, denaturation
🔴 High Priority
Immune system — innate vs adaptive, antibodies, MHC I/II, T & B cells
🔴 High Priority
Lab techniques — PCR, gel electrophoresis, ELISA, Western blot, CRISPR
🔴 High Priority
Signal transduction — G-protein coupled receptors, kinase cascades, second messengers
🟡 Medium
Circulatory system — heart anatomy, cardiac cycle, blood pressure
🟡 Medium
Endocrine system — hormones, feedback loops, diabetes, thyroid disorders
🟡 Medium
Renal system — nephron anatomy, filtration, acid-base regulation
🟡 Medium
Evolution & population genetics — Hardy-Weinberg, natural selection, speciation
⚪ Lower

Section 4: Psych/Soc (P/S)

59 questions · 95 minutes · Scored 118–132

▾
📌 Added to MCAT in 2015 — many pre-meds underestimate this section. The highest-yield area is social determinants of health. As a future bilingual pathologist serving the Central Valley, this material resonates with your experience.
Social determinants of health — income, education, race, access to care
🔴 High Priority
Health disparities — racial, ethnic, socioeconomic gaps in health outcomes
🔴 High Priority
Psychological disorders — DSM categories, symptoms, biopsychosocial model
🔴 High Priority
Learning & memory — classical vs operant conditioning, cognitive theories
🔴 High Priority
Perception & cognition — sensation, attention, memory encoding/retrieval
🟡 Medium
Social behavior — attitudes, conformity, group dynamics, stereotyping
🟡 Medium
Neuroscience — neurotransmitters, brain regions, nervous system anatomy
🟡 Medium
Research methods — experimental design, statistics, validity, reliability
🟡 Medium
Theories of development — Erikson, Piaget, Kohlberg, Vygotsky
⚪ Lower
